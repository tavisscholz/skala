# The Next Ten Locations: How to Scale a Multi-Unit Business Without Breaking Operations

2026-09-19

*Why the stretch from unit three to unit ten feels harder than the stretch from zero to three, and what separates operators who break through it from operators who get stuck in it.*

**Field Note**

You did the hard part. Unit one, you built with your own hands. Unit two and three, you ran on hustle: eighteen-hour days, personally training every manager, personally answering every 11pm call. It worked. Now you're staring at unit four or five, and something's off. It doesn't feel like the business is compounding. It feels like it's fraying. Margins are softer than they were. Turnover's up. You're more tired for less result than you were a year ago.

You haven't done anything wrong. You've hit a stage that has a name, and it hits almost everyone at almost the same spot.

## The Dead Space Between Three and Five Units

Consultants who study multi-unit operators call the zone between roughly three and five units the "dead space." Below three units, one hands-on owner can cover everything. Above five or six, you've usually built (or been forced to build) enough management layer to run the business without being in every room. In between, you've outgrown what you can personally do, but you haven't built what should replace you yet.

> **"The greatest risk in multi-unit franchising is not growing too quickly. It is becoming trapped between operator scale and enterprise scale."**
> *(Mellon Franchising)*

That's the trap in one sentence. It's not that you grew too fast. It's that your management infrastructure didn't grow with you.

**Fast Facts**
> - Between roughly 3 and 5 units, symptoms show up as rising complexity, manager turnover, slower execution, and margin pressure, even while revenue is climbing.
> - Franchisors stop supporting you at the four walls. Above-store infrastructure, shared services, recruiting, accounting, is entirely on you to build.
> - Operators in the 11 to 25 unit tier grew 33% over the last 15 years, and the 26 to 50 unit tier grew 55%, compared to 18.6% growth for operators stuck at 2 to 5 units. Scale, once the infrastructure exists, compounds on itself.

## The Unit-3 Failure Mode

Here's exactly how the dead space breaks businesses, and it's specific enough to recognize. An owner hires a GM for unit one, runs unit two personally, and opens unit three at the same time. What happens next is predictable: unit one's revenue dips 8 to 12% during the unit-three build-out because the owner's attention is split. Unit two, which never got the owner's full focus to begin with, plateaus 10 to 15% below what it should be doing.

Nobody did anything wrong. The math just didn't work, because one person was doing three jobs at once.

The fix is boring and it's the whole point: hire the area manager *before* you open unit three, not after you feel the pain of not having one.

> **The Unit-3 Fix**
> The salary you're avoiding is $75,000 to $95,000 a year. Treat it as a bet, not an expense: a bet that unit three actually works instead of producing three underperforming units where you used to have two strong ones. Operators who try to save that salary by managing everything themselves consistently get the worse outcome, not the cheaper one.

Unit two typically doesn't turn profitable until 12 to 18 months after opening, and during that stretch it needs $100,000 to $200,000 in working capital, usually pulled straight out of unit one's cash flow. That's a dependency loop, and it's exactly why the timing of your hires and your financing matters more than the timing of your leases.

## Two Camps, One Shared Truth

There's a real debate in this space, worth knowing so you can place yourself. One camp argues you should accelerate *through* the dead space, because additional unit count is what generates the revenue base that eventually pays for the management layer. Slowing down just prolongs the pain. The other camp argues the opposite: build the systems and leadership readiness first, because roughly 70% of multi-unit development agreements stall out when leadership and systems aren't in place before the growth commitment is signed.

Both camps actually agree on the variable that matters. It isn't speed. It's whether organizational infrastructure is being built in step with unit count, or lagging behind it. Fast growth with the infrastructure in place works. Fast growth without it produces exactly the unit-3 failure mode above, just at a bigger scale.

## The Money Got Easier in 2026

One thing changed this year that's worth knowing if you're financing the next few units: the SBA doubled its combined 7(a) and 504 lending cap from $5 million to $10 million, effective July 2026. That means a franchisee can now hold $5 million in 7(a) operating debt and $5 million in 504 real estate debt at the same time, a structure that wasn't possible before. Lenders are also increasingly building portfolio-level structures where unit one's performance pre-approves financing for unit two on better terms. If your unit-1 numbers are strong and documented, you have more leverage right now than operators had two years ago. Use it.

## What Building Ahead of Need Actually Looks Like

Jim D'Aquila, who built a multi-brand portfolio (Shipley Do-Nuts, Saigon Hustle, Sweet Paris) starting at age 62, put it plainly: *"We made a deliberate choice to build systems and infrastructure ahead of need. From our tech stack to our corporate structure to our talent bench, everything is designed for where we will be in five years, not just where we are today."* That's not a personality trait. It's a purchasing decision, made before the pressure forces it.

## Three Things That Aren't True

**"A strong first unit proves the model."** It proves the concept works once. It says nothing about whether the model works when you're not personally in the building.

**"Unit one's cash flow will fund expansion."** It will, until it can't. That dependency loop is exactly what strands operators mid-growth.

**"The next ten will be as hard as the last three."** The data says the opposite. Growth compounds once the infrastructure exists. The hard part is building the infrastructure, not sustaining the growth after.

> **Do This Week**
> Run the district-manager math before you sign the next lease. Take your current unit-1 and unit-2 margins and subtract a $75,000 to $95,000 area manager salary. If what's left still cash-flows positive, you're funded to build the layer you need. If it doesn't, that's the gap to close, in that order, before the next unit, not after it opens and starts bleeding your attention from the units you already have.
