Before You Sign the Lease: Why a Great Site Can Still Be the Wrong Location

Evaluate the trade area, the portfolio fit, and the unit economics. Not just the address.

Tavis Scholz · 19 September 2026 · 5 min read

The corner is perfect. Good visibility, strong traffic, the right neighbors, a landlord who wants you. Everybody who walks it says the same thing. And you can still lose money there for nine years, because the address is only one of the questions a lease answers, and it’s the one operators spend the most time on.

The lease is the least reversible decision you’ll make. Staff, pricing, hours, product mix, all of that can change in a week. Square footage on a nine-year term can’t. One in four new establishments doesn’t reach its first birthday. Some of those were bad concepts. A lot of them were good concepts in the wrong box.

Two sites on the same street can differ by 30% or more in passing traffic because of a corner, a crossing, or a bus stop. So measure the address, not the neighbourhood.

Six Checks, In This Order

The order matters. Cheap checks first, so you disqualify weak sites before you pay a lawyer or a surveyor. Any one of these can rule a site out.

Footfall at the door. Twelve months of it, not one. A street that lives off a summer market or a single office tower has a traffic profile that collapses for part of the year. Your rent doesn’t.

The real trade area. Where your customers actually come from is almost never a neat radius. It follows roads and transit and habit, and it stops dead at a river, a highway, or a rival cluster. Draw drive-time polygons, not circles. The primary trade area typically supplies 50 to 80% of revenue.

Demographics against your customer. Write your customer profile down before you look at the data, or you’ll talk yourself into whatever the site happens to show. Set pass-or-fail numbers: household income above a threshold at a minimum share of the trade area, residents within a ten-minute walk, enough daytime workers to carry a Tuesday.

The competitive set. Direct competitors inside the primary trade area, counted, not felt.

Accessibility. Visibility, ingress and egress, parking, transit.

The lease clauses themselves. Which is where good sites go bad.

Where Good Sites Go Bad

An office lease negotiates the box: square footage, rent, term. A retail lease negotiates the ecosystem: who else is in the center, how traffic reaches your door, whether you can grow nearby, and what share of your sales the landlord takes on top of rent. Those provisions can’t be unwound at renewal, so they deserve disproportionate attention now.

Fast facts

  • Percentage rent typically runs 4 to 12% of gross sales over a breakpoint. Negotiate the breakpoint 10 to 25% above the natural one, and narrow what counts as gross sales: exclude returns, sales tax, gift cards at issuance, third-party delivery commissions, e-commerce fulfilled elsewhere.
  • CAM and triple-net charges run $3 to $10 per square foot a year, higher in enclosed malls. Rent-to-sales for most retail formats needs to land under 8 to 12%.
  • Without an exclusive-use clause, the landlord can lease the suite next door to a direct competitor. Ask for exclusivity on your primary category with a radius and a real remedy.
  • Without a co-tenancy clause, the anchor can leave and your rent stays. Tie the rent obligation to minimum occupancy or named anchors, with rent relief and a termination right if they go dark.

Done well, percentage rent aligns you and the landlord. Done poorly, with a low breakpoint and a broad definition of sales, it makes the lease far more expensive than the base rent suggests. Get the property, finance, operations, and legal reads before the lease process starts, in a deal memo, not a phone call.

The Question That Decides the Deal

If you already operate nearby, there’s a check the six above don’t cover. A new location posts real revenue. If a chunk of it walked over from your store three miles away, the brand didn’t grow. It redistributed.

Net-New Sales

Model the proposed site’s trade area against every existing store nearby and estimate the shared customers. That gives you a transfer rate: the share of projected sales that’s a hand-off, not a gain. Subtract it and you have net-new sales, the single most useful number in the analysis. A site that posts strong gross sales but transfers half of them from an existing store is a very different investment than one with the same gross and almost no transfer. Typical infill openings run 5 to 8% cannibalization. Sometimes accepting more is right, to block a competitor or improve convenience. The failure isn’t overlap. It’s unmeasured overlap.

Score all of it against your best existing stores. If you can’t explain why your top performers work in these terms, you can’t tell whether the new site will.

Four things worth un-learning:

  • A great address is a great site. Measure the door, not the neighborhood.
  • The rent is the rent. Percentage rent, CAM, exclusives, and co-tenancy are the real occupancy cost.
  • Strong footfall means strong sales. Traffic that converts matters. Volume without fit is a trap.
  • A busy summer street is a good site. Test twelve months, not the peak.

This week

Before you look at another site, write down your customer on one page: income band, household type, how far they’ll travel, what day and hour they show up. Then take your best-performing store and run it through the six checks above. Write down the numbers it clears. That’s your pass-or-fail sheet, and any site that can’t clear what your best store clears isn’t a candidate, no matter how good the corner looks.
