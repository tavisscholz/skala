Why New Store Openings Fall Behind: 5 Handoffs Your Checklist Must Control

The owners, dependencies, and decision dates that keep routine surprises from becoming opening delays.

Tavis Scholz · 19 September 2026 · 5 min read

The opening date was set the day the lease got signed. Everybody agreed to it. There’s a checklist, a long one, and every line on it will get done. And the store will still open three weeks late, because the checklist never had the one thing that actually decides an opening date: who owns each handoff, what it depends on, and the date by which a decision has to be made.

Most openings run 90 to 180 days from lease signing to doors open. About 40% of them slip. When you go back and look at where the time went, it’s almost never the construction crew. It’s the space between teams.

Every boundary between teams is a risk point.

Hubler

A typical opening moves through roughly three dozen milestones and touches 15 to 20 people across real estate, legal, construction, IT, ops, HR, and the landlord’s side. Each time the work passes from one of them to the next, it costs two to five days on average. Five handoffs decide most of the schedule. Here they are, with what your checklist needs to say about each.

1. Lease to Pre-Construction: The Site Handover

A signed lease does not mean the site is ready for work. Delays start here because the premises condition, landlord obligations, access rights, permit status, and utility connections never got validated before the schedule got locked.

Treat the handover like a delivery you’re inspecting. Possession date. Loading access and delivery restrictions. Work-hour limits. Ceiling heights, floor condition, storefront condition. HVAC, electrical, and plumbing capacity. Fire and sprinkler coverage. Signage rights. Landlord-controlled areas. Have real estate, construction, ops, IT, and facilities all look at it, and get the answers in writing.

Fast facts

  • Every landlord dependency gets the same rigor as an internal task: an owner, a due date, an evidence requirement, and an escalation path. Not a verbal assurance.
  • The lease terms that become project inputs: rent-free period, fit-out commencement date, fit-out specifications, landlord approval requirements.
  • Leases that go into a filing cabinet create the problems that surface eighteen months later. Put the obligations somewhere the project team can see them.

2. Design to Permits: The Third-Party Clock

Permitting is the single largest source of delay, for three reasons: it happens before construction can start, it depends on somebody else’s review queue, and it varies wildly by jurisdiction. The same tenant build-out clears in four to eight weeks in a fast suburb and takes eight to twelve or more in a major coastal city.

The control here is a permit tracker, owned by someone on your side, not assumed to be handled by the general contractor. Each required approval, who’s responsible, submission date, expected approval date, what it depends on, current status, and the trigger that escalates it. Building, electrical, plumbing, signage, health, fire, occupancy, food handling, business registration. Whatever your format needs.

3. Procurement to Construction: Long-Lead Equipment

Rooftop HVAC units and electrical switchgear have run twenty to forty weeks to procure in recent years. That breaks the old assumption that you order them once construction is underway. Signage and landlord deliverables round out the usual hiding places. All of these run on their own clock, in parallel with the build, which is exactly why they get missed.

The Fixture Tracker

Fixtures connect construction to merchandising. Gondolas, cashwraps, refrigeration cases, signage frames, specialty equipment. They have to arrive on time, undamaged, and in the right sequence, or merchandising setup gets compressed into overnight recovery work. Track order date, vendor, ship date, delivery window, installation owner, quality inspection, and missing or damaged items. The decision date is the day the order has to be placed to hit the install window, not the day construction needs it.

4. Construction to Operations: The Readiness Gate

Two mistakes live here. Delaying the opening over a scratched wall, and opening with a failed fire inspection. The punch list needs to distinguish opening-critical defects from post-opening corrections, and it needs to do that before the final walkthrough, not during it.

Define who has authority to sign off on construction completion, IT, security, merchandising, operations, HR, compliance, and the final go or no-go. Then require evidence, not confidence: inspection certificates, test results, photos, training records, inventory reports, system screenshots, vendor completion forms. If leadership decides to open with something unresolved, the rationale and the mitigation plan get written down. That protects the business from informal risk acceptance.

5. Project Team to Store Team: Day One

The opening sequence itself is a chain: signage, final inspections, certificate of occupancy, punch list, merchandising, staff training, soft open, grand open. The last handoff is the project team handing the building to the people who’ll run it. Put store associates in the final walkthrough. They think in terms of daily use and catch what headquarters misses. It also gives them ownership before the doors open.

Then remember the opening isn’t the finish line. New stores typically take 12 to 24 months to reach maturity, and the first 90 days separate the ones that get there from the ones that stall.

Why It Keeps Happening

There’s no single source of truth. The project plan lives in someone’s spreadsheet. Vendor contracts are in email threads. The compliance checklist is in someone’s drawer. Finance doesn’t know the fit-out is over budget until month end. Ops doesn’t know the fire approval is pending until opening week.

Openings run by a project manager with a standardized playbook finish on average 43 days faster than ones run ad hoc. Not because the PM is a hero. Because every dependency carries an owner, a date, an evidence requirement, and an escalation path, so control survives whoever’s in the seat.

Three things worth un-learning:

  • The checklist is the plan. A checklist without owners, dependencies, and decision dates is a document, not a control system.
  • The general contractor owns permits. Permits run on a third-party queue. You need a tracker, not an assumption.
  • Delays are construction’s fault. Most delays originate in the handoffs and the third-party clocks, not in how fast the crew works.

This week

Open your current opening checklist. For every line, add three columns: who owns it, what it can’t start without, and the date by which a decision must be made or the opening date moves. Then look for the lines that have no owner, or where the owner is “the landlord” or “the GC.” Those are your next three weeks of delay, and you can see them today.
